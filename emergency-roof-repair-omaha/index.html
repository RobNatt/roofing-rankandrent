What counts as a roof emergency

Call for an emergency repair when water is getting into the house or the roof is open to the weather. That usually means one of these:

  • Water dripping or running through a ceiling, light fixture or wall during rain or snowmelt.
  • Shingles torn off by wind so you can see felt paper or bare wood decking.
  • A tree limb or other debris that has punched through the roof.
  • A skylight, vent or chimney cap broken by hail or wind.
  • A ceiling that is sagging or bulging with water.

Some things look alarming but can wait for a normal appointment: a dry stain that has not grown, a few shingle granules in the gutter, or one cracked shingle with no water inside. For those, see roof leak repair instead.

What to do in the first hour

  1. Stay off the roof. A wet or icy roof is dangerous even for people who do this every day. Leave the climbing to the contractor.
  2. Protect people first. Keep everyone out of any room where the ceiling is bulging. If water is reaching lights or outlets, switch off that circuit at the breaker panel if you can reach it safely and the floor around the panel is dry.
  3. Catch the water. Put down buckets and towels, and move furniture, rugs and electronics out of the way.
  4. Take photos and video. Record the damage inside and outside before anything is moved or patched. The Nebraska Department of Insurance (hail damage guidance, accessed 2026) tells homeowners to photograph or video damage if repairs have to happen before the adjuster comes, and says you are responsible for taking steps to prevent further damage.
  5. Keep receipts. Tarps, buckets, a wet-vac rental and emergency labor are all costs you may want to show your insurer.
  6. Call for help. Call (402) 772-6658 and we will pass your request to a local roofing contractor. If a storm caused the damage, call your insurance company or agent as well.

What an emergency roof repair usually includes

The goal of an emergency visit is to stop water getting in, not to rebuild the roof that night. A typical emergency repair is one or more of these:

  • Tarping. A heavy tarp laid over the damaged area, running up and over the ridge where possible, and held down with boards fastened so the wind cannot get under it.
  • Small patch repairs. Replacing a few blown-off shingles, resealing lifted flashing or covering a punctured spot when the weather allows.
  • Debris removal. Getting a fallen limb off the roof so the damage can be covered.

The permanent repair comes later, after a proper inspection in daylight and dry weather. Ask the contractor to photograph the damage and the temporary fix so you have a record for your insurer.

Why timing matters in Omaha

In spring and summer, Omaha thunderstorms can come through several times in a week, and a torn section that lets in a little water today can let in a lot more in the next storm. In winter, snow that melts and refreezes at the cold eaves can form an ice dam that pushes water under shingles, and freeze and thaw cycles widen small gaps. Some permanent repairs are harder in deep cold, so a sound temporary repair is worth having.

What emergency repairs cost

Every job is different, so only the contractor who sees your roof can price it. As a rough guide, This Old House's roof repair cost guide (updated 2026) lists typical national ranges of $100 to $1,500 for holes and leaks and $200 to $500 for flashing repairs, and notes that emergency service can add several hundred dollars. If storm damage is covered by your homeowners policy, the cost of reasonable temporary repairs may be part of the claim. Check your policy and ask your agent.

Nebraska rules that protect you

Storms bring out door-knockers. If you sign a contract with a roofer that will be paid from insurance money, Nebraska law gives you some protection:

  • You can cancel that contract in writing until midnight on the third business day after you sign it, or after your insurer tells you in writing that all or part of the claim is not covered, whichever is later. The contractor must return your payments within ten days, but can still charge the reasonable value of emergency work you agreed to in writing (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8603).
  • A contractor may not offer to pay or rebate your insurance deductible to win the job (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8604).
  • Before starting repair work on an insured loss, the contractor must give you and your insurer an itemized breakdown of the work, materials, labor and total price (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8606).

Read every page before signing, especially anything that assigns your insurance benefits to the contractor. Our hail damage claim guide explains assignments in plain English.
