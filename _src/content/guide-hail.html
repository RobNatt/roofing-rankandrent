This guide explains the general process in Nebraska. It is not legal or insurance advice. Your own policy wording decides what is covered, so read it and talk to your agent. Figures and rules below link to their sources, with the year we checked them.

The short version

  1. Make sure everyone is safe and stop water getting in.
  2. Check for signs of real hail damage, starting at ground level.
  3. Photograph everything before anything is repaired.
  4. Get the roof inspected and check your wind and hail deductible.
  5. If the damage is real and worth claiming, report it to your insurer promptly.
  6. Meet the adjuster, compare their estimate with your roofer's, and ask about differences.
  7. Understand the first payment (actual cash value) and how to recover the rest (replacement cost) after the work is done.
  8. Hire carefully and know your Nebraska rights before you sign.

Step 1: Stay safe and stop more damage

After a storm, do not climb onto the roof. Hail-damaged roofs can be slick and the edges can be weakened. If water is coming in, catch it, move belongings and keep people out of any room with a sagging ceiling. Then arrange a temporary repair such as a tarp. The Nebraska Department of Insurance (accessed 2026) says homeowners are responsible for taking any necessary steps to prevent further damage to their property. Keep receipts for tarps, materials and emergency labor, because reasonable costs to protect the home may be part of the claim. Our emergency roof repair page covers the first hour in more detail.

Step 2: Check whether hail really damaged the roof

Not every hail storm damages a roof, and filing a claim for damage that is not there wastes your time and can still show up on your insurance record. The Department of Insurance's hail guide gives some useful rules of thumb:

  • Size. Hail generally must be about 1¼ inches in diameter (half-dollar size) to damage heavy composite or wood shake shingles. Lightweight composite shingles can be damaged by hail around 1 inch (quarter size). Hail under 1 inch usually only damages shingles that are already deteriorated.
  • Ground-level clues. Hail strong enough to damage a roof will usually also damage cars, wood fences, shutters and siding nearby. Check those first.
  • Pattern. Hail makes a random scatter of hits. A regular pattern suggests some other cause.
  • Bruising. A hail hit typically leaves a bruise in the shingle that you can feel as a soft spot.
  • Granules. Granules collecting at the downspouts may be a sign, but granule loss alone does not prove hail damage.

The department also warns that age-related cracking, curling, blistering, algae or lichen and manufacturing defects are often confused with hail. If you are unsure, the department suggests talking to your agent or company about getting an inspection.

Step 3: Document everything before repairs

Good records make a claim go faster and help settle disagreements. Before anything is cleaned up or fixed:

  • Write down the date and approximate time of the storm.
  • Photograph hail on the ground with a coin or ruler for scale, if it has not melted yet.
  • Photograph the roof from the ground, dents in gutters, downspouts and roof vents, damaged siding, window screens and fences, and any dented cars.
  • Photograph and video any water damage inside: ceilings, walls, floors, furniture.
  • Keep damaged items such as wet carpet or furniture for the adjuster to see if you can. The department specifically asks homeowners to retain damaged items for the adjuster before throwing them out.

If repairs cannot wait for the adjuster, the department says to take video or good quality photographs of the damage first.

Step 4: Get an inspection and check your deductible

A roofer can get up on the roof safely, check sections of it closely for hail hits and give you a straight answer on whether there is storm damage and roughly what fixing it involves. Ask for photos of what they find.

Then find your declarations page and look at your deductible. The Department of Insurance notes that some companies charge a higher deductible for wind and hail damage than for other kinds of damage. Some policies set the wind and hail deductible as a percentage of the home's insured value rather than a flat dollar amount, which can make it much larger than you expect.

EXAMPLE This is made-up arithmetic to show how a percentage deductible works, not a quote. If a home is insured for $300,000 and the policy has a 1% wind and hail deductible, the deductible is $300,000 × 1% = $3,000. If the roofer thinks the repair will cost about $2,000, a claim would pay nothing, because the repair is less than the deductible.

The department's advice is simple: if you believe the repairs will cost less than your deductible, consider not filing a claim. It also points out that some insurers take wind and hail claims into account at renewal, even when no payment was made.

Step 5: Report the claim promptly

If there is real damage and it is likely to cost more than your deductible, contact your insurance company or agent as soon as you can. Your policy will have its own rules about how and when to report a loss, so check it. When you call, have your policy number, the date of the storm, a short description of the damage and your photos ready. Write down the claim number and the name of everyone you speak to.

Step 6: The adjuster's inspection

The insurance company will send an adjuster to look at the damage. According to the Department of Insurance, you may not need to be there for the inspection, but many homeowners like to be, and some invite their roofer too. Point out everything you think was damaged, including items people forget: windows, siding, fascia, gutters, ceilings and satellite dishes.

The adjuster writes an estimate of the cost to repair the damage. If your roofer's estimate is different, the department's advice is to let your adjuster know. Often the difference comes down to materials, the amount of damage found, or items one side missed, and it can be explained or corrected.

Step 7: How the payments work

This is the part that confuses most people. Many Nebraska homeowners policies pay a roof claim in two stages.

Actual cash value first

The Department of Insurance explains that the adjuster estimates the cost of repairs and works out the roof's actual cash value just before the storm. Actual cash value is the replacement cost minus depreciation for the roof's age and wear. Your deductible is subtracted, and that amount is usually the first check.

Replacement cost after the work is done

If your policy has replacement cost coverage, you may be able to collect the difference between the actual cash value payment and the full replacement cost once the repairs are made. The department says these repairs must be made within a specific amount of time, usually 180 days. Check your own policy for the exact deadline and what proof the insurer needs, such as a final invoice.

EXAMPLE Made-up numbers to show the math, not a quote or a promise of payment. Say the adjuster puts the full replacement cost at $15,000 and takes $5,000 off for depreciation. Actual cash value is $15,000 − $5,000 = $10,000. With a $2,000 deductible, the first check is $10,000 − $2,000 = $8,000. If the policy pays replacement cost, the $5,000 held back for depreciation may be paid once the roof is replaced within the policy's deadline. Total paid: $8,000 + $5,000 = $13,000, which is the $15,000 replacement cost minus your $2,000 deductible.

If your mortgage company is named on the policy, the check may be made out to both you and the lender. Call the lender early to ask how they release roof repair funds.

Step 8: Hire carefully and know your rights

After a big Omaha hail storm, roofers come from all over the region. Before you sign anything, check these Nebraska rules:

  • Three-day cancellation. If you sign a contract with a residential contractor that is to be paid from your property insurance, you can cancel it in writing until midnight on the later of the third business day after signing, or the third business day after your insurer tells you in writing that all or part of the claim is not covered. The contractor must return payments within ten days, though it may keep the reasonable value of emergency work you agreed to in writing (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8603, enacted 2012).
  • No deductible deals. A contractor may not promise to pay or rebate any part of your deductible, including through discounts or other payments, to get your business (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8604, enacted 2012). If someone offers to "eat your deductible", walk away.
  • Assignment of benefits rules. An assignment hands some of your rights under the policy to the contractor. Nebraska requires these to include a capitalized 14-point warning, a disclosure that the contractor cannot guarantee the loss will be fully covered, and delivery to your insurer within five business days of signing (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8605, enacted 2018). Read it carefully and ask questions before signing one.
  • Itemized breakdown. Before starting work on an insured loss, the contractor must give you and your insurer an itemized list of the work, materials, labor, fees and total price (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8606, enacted 2018).
  • Registration. Look the company up in the Nebraska Department of Labor contractor registration search.
  • Permits. Ask whether the job needs a permit and who will pull it. For work that needs one, the City of Omaha says you must have an approved permit before construction begins (City of Omaha Planning Department).

If you disagree with the insurer

Start by asking the adjuster to explain the estimate in writing, and send them your roofer's estimate and photos. Many differences get resolved this way. If you believe the company did not follow your policy, you can file a complaint with the Nebraska Department of Insurance or talk to a personal attorney. The department notes that it is an administrative agency, not a court, so it cannot decide factual questions such as whether hail or something else caused the damage. It can review whether the insurer followed Nebraska insurance law. Its consumer hotline listed on the hail guide is 877-564-7323 (in-state).

Should you upgrade while you are replacing?

If the roof is being replaced, ask about impact-resistant shingles. The Department of Insurance says rebuilding with impact-resistant roofing or siding may qualify you for discounts on your homeowners policy. Ask your agent how much, because it varies. Upgrades beyond what the policy covers are usually paid by you, so get the price difference in writing. Our roof replacement cost guide shows typical price ranges.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting until the roof leaks to check for hail damage.
  • Throwing away damaged items before the adjuster sees them.
  • Signing a contract or assignment on the doorstep the day after the storm.
  • Forgetting the replacement cost deadline and losing the depreciation holdback.
  • Paying a large deposit up front to a contractor you cannot find in the state registration search.
