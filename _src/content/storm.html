Hail season in Omaha

Most of Omaha's damaging hail and wind comes with spring and summer thunderstorms. A single storm can hit one side of the city hard and miss the other entirely, which is why one street may be full of roofing signs while the next has none. Hail damage is also easy to miss from the ground. Bruised shingles can look fine from the driveway and start leaking months later, after the claim deadline on some policies has passed.

How to tell whether hail damaged your roof

The Nebraska Department of Insurance's guide "Hail Damage: Does My Roof Need Repair?" (accessed 2026) gives practical rules of thumb:

  • Hail size matters. The department says hail generally must be about 1¼ inches across (roughly half-dollar size) to damage heavy composite or wood shake shingles, and around 1 inch (quarter size) for lightweight composite shingles. Smaller hail usually only marks shingles that are already worn out.
  • Look at ground level first. Hail strong enough to damage a roof usually also dents cars, gutters, wood fences, shutters and siding.
  • Look for a random pattern. Hail strikes are scattered. Damage that forms a neat pattern points to another cause.
  • Feel for bruises. A hail hit often leaves a soft spot in the shingle that you can feel with your fingers.
  • Granules alone are not proof. Some granules in the gutters are normal on older roofs.

The department also lists things that are often mistaken for hail: cracking, curling and blistering from age, algae or lichen, and manufacturing defects. A good roofer will tell you honestly which one you are looking at.

Wind damage

Straight-line winds in Omaha thunderstorms can be as damaging as hail. Look for missing shingles or ridge caps, shingles that are creased or folded back, and debris on the roof. A creased shingle has usually lost its seal and can blow off in the next storm even if it is still there today.

What to do after a storm, in order

  1. Stay safe and stop water getting in. If the roof is open, see emergency roof repair. The insurance department says you are responsible for preventing further damage.
  2. Document everything. Photograph the roof from the ground, dented gutters and vents, damaged cars and siding, and any water inside. Note the date and time of the storm.
  3. Get a roof inspection. A roofer can check the roof up close and tell you whether there is real storm damage before you decide to file.
  4. Check your deductible. Many policies have a separate, higher deductible for wind and hail. If repairs will cost less than your deductible, the department suggests you may not want to file.
  5. Contact your insurer or agent. If there is real damage, report it promptly. An adjuster will inspect the roof.

Storm contractor checklist

After big hail storms, out-of-town crews often go door to door. Many do fine work, but some disappear when problems show up later. Before you sign with anyone:

  • Look them up in the Nebraska Department of Labor registered contractor search. Nebraska requires contractors and subcontractors doing business in the state to register.
  • Ask for a local address and phone number and proof of liability insurance and workers' compensation.
  • Ask whether the job needs a permit and who will pull it. The City of Omaha's permit page explains the city's process.
  • Never let a contractor promise to "cover" or "eat" your deductible. That is illegal in Nebraska (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8604).
  • Be careful with any form that assigns your insurance benefits to the contractor. Nebraska law sets rules for these, including a large-print warning and delivery to your insurer within five business days (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8605).
  • Know your right to cancel an insurance-funded contract within three business days of signing, or of a written claim denial (Neb. Rev. Stat. 44-8603).

Thinking about impact-resistant shingles?

If your roof needs replacing after a hail storm, ask about impact-resistant shingles. The Nebraska Department of Insurance notes that rebuilding with impact-resistant roofing may qualify you for a discount on your homeowners policy.
